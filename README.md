# What this paper is, and how it came from your `results-2.json`

This explains what got written, where every number came from, what the paper
actually claims, and — just as important — what it deliberately does **not**
claim.

## 1. Where the numbers come from

Every table and figure in `paper.tex` / `paper.pdf` was generated by
`analysis.py` (included) reading directly from your uploaded
`results-2.json` — the file produced by the "standard" mode run of
`TINYBENCH_colab_v2.ipynb` on Google Colab. Nothing in the tables or the
prose was invented, estimated, or filled in from general knowledge of how
compression "usually" behaves.

That file only contains **one completed training seed** (seed 0). Seeds 1
and 2 never ran because the whole pass took 83 minutes and the notebook's
time budget stopped there. Every number in the paper is from that single
seed. This is stated explicitly in the paper (abstract, Section III-E,
Section V) rather than hidden.

## 2. What the paper actually found

**Quantization (clean comparison — same weights, just a different numeric
format):**
- FP16 barely touched predictions: 0%, 0.03%, 0.12% changed on HAR /
  Fashion-MNIST / CIFAR-10.
- Full-integer int8 changed more predictions (0.31% / 0.73% / 1.79%) than
  accuracy alone would suggest — e.g. on CIFAR-10, 74 predictions flipped
  correct→wrong and 32 flipped wrong→correct, netting out to a 0.42-point
  accuracy change that hides 106 individual flips.
- An exact statistical test (McNemar) shows some of these flip patterns are
  not just noise (Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10 full-int8; Fashion-MNIST
  dynamic-range), even after correcting for testing multiple comparisons.

**Weight clustering (16 shared values per kernel, no fine-tuning):**
- Changed 1.5% / 12.9% / 38.2% of predictions — much larger than
  quantization, and this is a clean comparison too (same underlying
  weights, no retraining involved).

**Pruning — ⚠️ confounded, and the paper says so upfront:**
- Accuracy *rose* after pruning on two of three datasets (e.g. the
  50%-pruned Fashion-MNIST model beat the dense teacher by 13 points).
  Pruning cannot make a model better — what actually happened is that the
  teachers were under-trained, and the fine-tuning epochs applied after
  pruning kept training them.
- Because of this, the paper explicitly refuses to claim "pruning improved
  accuracy" or to compare pruned-vs-teacher agreement. It reports what
  *can* be trusted here: sparsity's effect on **compressed file size**
  (very real: 90% sparsity → 22–25% of the dense gzip size) and, among the
  three pruned variants that all had the *same* fine-tuning budget, that
  accuracy still fell monotonically with sparsity.
- The paper names the missing experiment that would fix this: a control
  model fine-tuned for the same epochs with 0% sparsity.

**Distillation — also affected by the under-trained teacher, disclosed:**
- Distilled students did not beat scratch-trained students in this run.
  The paper reports this plainly rather than searching for a way to spin
  it positive, and explains why it can't be read as "distillation doesn't
  work" (untuned hyperparameters, weak teachers, one seed).

**Latency — the paper treats this as an experiment in its own right:**
- The full latency sweep was run twice. A third of the 90 paired medians
  disagreed by more than 1.5x between the two runs, with some pairs
  disagreeing by 10–40x. This is reported as a finding (measurement
  reliability on a shared cloud CPU), not smoothed over.
- Within that noise: students were consistently faster than the teacher;
  int8 was never measurably faster than FP32 on this hardware; pruning
  gave no measurable latency benefit (expected, since the exported model
  is still dense — TFLite doesn't skip stored zeros).

## 3. What the paper refuses to claim, and why

This is the part most likely to matter for review:

| Claim a weaker paper might make | Why this paper doesn't make it |
|---|---|
| "Pruning improves accuracy" | The teacher was still converging; the fine-tuning step, not the pruning, likely caused the gain. Stated explicitly in §IV-D and §V. |
| "Distillation underperforms scratch training, in general" | Untuned T/α, one seed, weak teachers. Stated in §IV-E and §V. |
| "int8 quantization is X times faster" | Never measurably faster on this CPU; the paper reports the range overlap instead of a fabricated speedup number. |
| Any claim from seeds 1–2 | They don't exist in the data. The paper says "single seed" throughout instead of implying replication. |
| Precise energy/RAM numbers | Never measured. Activation memory is labeled explicitly as an *analytic estimate* from tensor shapes, not a measurement. |

A full "Limitations and Threats to Validity" section (Section V) collects
all of this in one place, as IEEE reviewers expect.

## 5. What you still need to do before submitting anywhere

I can't make these calls for you, and no honest tool can:

1. **Fill in author names, affiliation, and emails** — currently
   `[Author 1]`, `[Department]`, etc. placeholders in `paper.tex`.
2. **Rewrite it in your own words.** I wrote this in a plain, direct
   research register on purpose (short sentences, no filler, no hedge-y
   AI-ish phrasing), but "make it read like you" is something only you can
   finish. Go through it and change phrasing that doesn't sound like your
   voice — this is also your real defense against AI-detection concerns,
   far more than any tool-level trick.
3. **Verify every reference yourself.** All 24 references are real,
   well-known papers in this area (Jacob et al. on integer-only inference,
   Han et al. on pruning and Deep Compression, Hinton et al. on
   distillation, the UCI HAR / Fashion-MNIST / CIFAR-10 dataset papers,
   etc.) — but I can't guarantee every page number and venue detail is
   letter-perfect, and a submission with a wrong citation detail is an
   easy, avoidable strike against you. Check each one against the source.
4. **Decide on a venue and check its specific requirements** — page
   limits, whether it wants `conference` or `journal` IEEEtran mode,
   supplementary material policy, double-blind requirements (if
   double-blind, you'll need to strip identifying info before review).
5. **Consider the seed-count gap before submitting**, honestly. A reviewer
   who reads Section V closely will likely ask for the pruning control and
   more seeds. You can either address that now (rerun with the fixes I
   described earlier) or submit as-is and expect that as a revision
   request. Both are legitimate calls — it's your decision, not mine to
   make silently by hiding the gap.

## 6. On "near-zero AI/plagiarism"

I can't promise a specific detector score — nobody honestly can, and I'd be
skeptical of any tool that claims to. What actually protects you:

- Every claim in the paper traces to a specific number in your data, not
  to generic phrasing about how compression "typically" behaves — that
  specificity is what makes text read as genuinely yours.
- The paper's honesty about its own confounds and its single-seed
  limitation is not just good science — it's also exactly the kind of
  content that's very hard for anything to have "generated" generically,
  because it's tied to the quirks of *this* run.
- Rewriting in your own voice (point 2 above) is the single biggest lever
  you have.
- The reference list is real, checkable literature — not invented
  citations — which is the most common and most damaging problem in
  AI-assisted paper writing.
