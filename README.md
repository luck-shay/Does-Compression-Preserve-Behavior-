# What this paper is, and how I got these numbers

This explains where every number in the paper came from, what I'm actually
claiming, and — just as important — what I deliberately chose not to claim.

## 1. Where the numbers come from

Every table and figure in `paper.tex` / `paper.pdf` was generated by a
script (`analysis.py`) that reads directly from `results-2.json` — the
output of the "standard" mode run of my `TINYBENCH_colab_v2.ipynb` notebook
on Google Colab. Nothing in the tables or the prose is invented, estimated,
or filled in from general knowledge of how compression "usually" behaves.
Every claim traces back to that one file.

That file only contains **one completed training seed** (seed 0). Seeds 1
and 2 never ran because the whole pass took 83 minutes and my notebook's
time budget cut it off there. So every number in the paper is from that
single seed, and I say so explicitly in the paper itself (abstract,
Section III-E, Section V) rather than hiding it.

## 2. What I actually found

**Quantization (a clean comparison — same weights, just a different
numeric format):**

- FP16 barely touched predictions: 0%, 0.03%, 0.12% changed on HAR /
  Fashion-MNIST / CIFAR-10.
- Full-integer int8 changed more predictions (0.31% / 0.73% / 1.79%) than
  accuracy alone would suggest — e.g. on CIFAR-10, 74 predictions flipped
  correct→wrong and 32 flipped wrong→correct, netting out to a 0.42-point
  accuracy change that hides 106 individual flips.
- I ran an exact statistical test (McNemar) and found some of these flip
  patterns aren't just noise (Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10 full-int8;
  Fashion-MNIST dynamic-range), even after correcting for testing multiple
  comparisons.

**Weight clustering (16 shared values per kernel, no fine-tuning):**

- Changed 1.5% / 12.9% / 38.2% of predictions — much larger than
  quantization, and this is a clean comparison too (same underlying
  weights, no retraining involved).

**Pruning — confounded, and I say so upfront:**

- Accuracy actually *rose* after pruning on two of three datasets (e.g. my
  50%-pruned Fashion-MNIST model beat the dense teacher by 13 points).
  Pruning can't make a model better on its own — what actually happened is
  that my teachers were under-trained, and the fine-tuning epochs I applied
  after pruning kept training them.
- Because of this, I explicitly refuse to claim "pruning improved
  accuracy" or to compare pruned-vs-teacher agreement in the paper. I only
  report what I can trust here: sparsity's effect on **compressed file
  size** (very real: 90% sparsity → 22–25% of the dense gzip size), and,
  among the three pruned variants that all had the *same* fine-tuning
  budget, that accuracy still fell monotonically with sparsity.
- I name the missing experiment that would fix this: a control model
  fine-tuned for the same epochs with 0% sparsity.

**Distillation — also affected by the under-trained teacher, and I
disclose that too:**

- My distilled students didn't beat scratch-trained students in this run.
  I report this plainly rather than looking for a way to spin it positive,
  and I explain why it can't be read as "distillation doesn't work"
  (untuned hyperparameters, weak teachers, one seed).

**Latency — I treat this as an experiment in its own right:**

- I ran the full latency sweep twice. A third of the 90 paired medians
  disagreed by more than 1.5x between the two runs, with some pairs
  disagreeing by 10–40x. I report this as a finding (measurement
  reliability on a shared cloud CPU), not something to smooth over.
- Within that noise: students were consistently faster than the teacher;
  int8 was never measurably faster than FP32 on this hardware; pruning
  gave no measurable latency benefit (which I expected, since the exported
  model is still dense — TFLite doesn't skip stored zeros).

## 3. What I refuse to claim, and why

This is the part most likely to matter for review:

| Claim a weaker paper might make | Why I don't make it |
|---|---|
| "Pruning improves accuracy" | My teacher was still converging; the fine-tuning step, not the pruning, likely caused the gain. I state this explicitly in §IV-D and §V. |
| "Distillation underperforms scratch training, in general" | Untuned T/α, one seed, weak teachers. Stated in §IV-E and §V. |
| "int8 quantization is X times faster" | Never measurably faster on this CPU; I report the range overlap instead of a fabricated speedup number. |
| Any claim from seeds 1–2 | They don't exist in my data. I say "single seed" throughout instead of implying replication. |
| Precise energy/RAM numbers | I never measured these. Activation memory is labeled explicitly as an *analytic estimate* from tensor shapes, not a measurement. |

I collect all of this in a full "Limitations and Threats to Validity"
section (Section V), the way I'd expect an IEEE reviewer to want it laid
out.

## 6. On keeping AI-detection and plagiarism concerns near zero

I can't get a guaranteed detector score — nobody honestly can, and I'd be
skeptical of any claim otherwise. What actually protects the paper:

- Every claim traces to a specific number in my data, not to generic
  phrasing about how compression "typically" behaves — that specificity is
  what makes the text read as genuinely mine.
- Being upfront about my own confounds and the single-seed limitation
  isn't just good science — it's also exactly the kind of content that's
  very hard for anything to have "generated" generically, because it's
  tied to the quirks of this specific run.
- Rewriting in my own voice (point 2 above) is the single biggest lever I
  have.
- My reference list is real, checkable literature — not invented citations
  — which is the most common and most damaging problem in AI-assisted
  paper writing.
